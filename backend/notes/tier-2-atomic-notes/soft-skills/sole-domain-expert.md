# Being the Sole Domain Expert: Frontend Developer on Integrations and Nexus

The clearest examples of being the only domain expert are Integrations and Nexus at Nurtur. On each project I was the only frontend developer for the entire duration. On Nexus it was me and one backend developer; even the senior developer overseeing the project from a distance was backend-only. On Integrations I was working alongside an entire team of experienced backend engineers in systems they'd known for years.

There were people I could escalate to when something was seriously wrong, but they weren't in the codebase day-to-day. Getting a second opinion meant someone putting in real effort to understand code they didn't know — it wasn't a casual lean-over-and-ask. For most decisions, I worked it out alone.

Over time this gave me a reputation as highly mobile. Frontend tasks that didn't justify a dedicated team came to me. Backend developers reached out informally with frontend questions. I moved between teams when projects needed frontend work done, which built wide context across different parts of the company.

The freedom was genuinely good — I worked the way I thought best. Nobody overrode architectural decisions I'd thought through carefully, and nobody introduced inconsistencies I'd have to reconcile later. Full ownership meant the quality of the codebase reflected my choices directly.

The harder part was learning mostly from my own mistakes rather than a peer's eye on the same code. A frontend colleague working in the same codebase naturally catches things — not just bugs, but simpler approaches, style inconsistencies, patterns they've seen work elsewhere. Without that I grew from experience but missed the compounding effect of someone else's perspective.

The specific thing I missed most was being able to share a clever solution with someone who'd understand what I'd done. Working out something elegant with no one to share it with is a different kind of satisfaction — complete, but quieter.
