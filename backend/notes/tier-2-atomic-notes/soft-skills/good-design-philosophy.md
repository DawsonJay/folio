# What Good Design Means: Invisible, Intuitive, and Answering Unspoken Questions

Good design answers the questions you have — including the ones you didn't know you had — and lets you take actions with barely a thought. It feels invisible, like there was no other way it could have been made. You know you're there when users can't tell you're doing it.

The clearest illustration I have is the Integrations Dashboard at Nurtur. The first version was an engineer-first pass: it got the data visible and truthful, which was the right early priority. But it didn't shape pages around how a salesperson actually scans information, prioritises tasks, or completes a workflow. Every page felt overwhelming. It wasn't answering the unspoken questions users arrived with. Good data, poor design.

A less professional example — but one that changed how I think — was helping a technophobic friend set up Discord. Something I found trivial took hours and genuinely stressed her out. Every obstacle made her feel stupid. Up until that point I'd been unconsciously designing for developers. After that I started designing for technophobes: if something feels frictionless to someone who finds technology upsetting, it's excellent for everyone else too. If I can make it feel natural and obvious to someone who would otherwise give up, I've solved the hard version of the problem.

Both experiences point at the same thing: good design isn't about providing all the information. It's about surfacing the right information, in the right order, so the user barely has to think. The difference between the engineer-first Integrations dashboard and the redesigned version was the same data, presented with hierarchy and context — traffic-light colours for statuses, layered screens showing what data was a subset of what, only the essential information at a glance with drill-down for everything else.

Good design feels inevitable — like there was no other way to do it. If users notice the design, it's usually because something is wrong.
