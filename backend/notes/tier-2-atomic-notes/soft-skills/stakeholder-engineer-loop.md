# Stakeholder-Engineer Alignment: Closing the Loop on Nexus

The most common failure mode between stakeholders and engineers is a gap that nobody is actively closing. Stakeholders have domain knowledge — they know what would make their work more effective — but can't translate that into engineering terms. Engineers understand what's feasible but don't always understand what's actually valuable to the people using the product. Without someone bridging those two, the product is shaped more by what's easy to build than by what's useful.

On Nexus I drove that bridging consistently. The sales team who would use it had good ideas grounded in real daily use, but they had no frontend knowledge and limited backend understanding. Their suggestions sometimes described solutions rather than problems, or assumed feasibility without knowing the effort involved.

Rather than accepting or rejecting ideas, I ran a consistent loop. After each round of suggestions, I'd investigate: mapping what the frontend would need, checking with my backend developer Craig on what his side would require. Then I'd come back with a clear picture: what was feasible, what wasn't, and what alternatives might deliver similar value. I asked which trade-offs mattered most to them.

A concrete example: the sales team suggested regex filtering for a series of entries. It sounded simple but involved significant frontend and backend work — infrastructure that didn't exist, edge cases to handle, API calls to redesign. I didn't refuse in the meeting. I investigated, got the full picture from both sides, and came back with options and trade-offs.

That loop produced better suggestions over time. Stakeholders built a working sense of what was hard versus easy, what had alternatives versus what was genuinely one-of-a-kind. It also meant I learned what was actually valuable, which changed how I prioritised what to build.

This wasn't a process handed to me. I saw the gap, saw that it cost the project quality, and built the habit of closing it. Projects like Nexus run on that rhythm constantly because someone has to keep closing the loop.
