# When Something Is Done: Definition and the "Could Be Better" Tension

Done means three things to me: it meets the specification, it's structurally stable — unlikely to develop serious bugs over time — and it's passed the relevant checks, whether that's peer review, tests, or QA. That's become as much instinct as checklist after enough years of working to that definition.

The harder question is "could be better." The answer is always yes. I handle that by forming a clear picture of the purpose before I start anything. What does this need to do? What problem does it solve? There's a point of diminishing returns where additional work stops serving the purpose and just adds complexity. Anything past that point is perfectionism, not improvement.

My art background is part of why that clicks. There's a recognisable moment in a painting where it's done — it communicates what you want it to, and anything else you add will move it away from that rather than toward it. Code has the same quality. The right stopping point is when the thing does its job cleanly and stably, not when you run out of ideas for making it more elaborate.

WhatNow is an honest example of the trade-off. I shipped it earlier than I'd have liked — the interface wasn't as polished as I'd normally want. I was making portfolio projects under time pressure. The internals were solid and clean; I used AI to generate the interface rather than doing it myself, which sacrificed something I care about — design — to protect what mattered more: a codebase that was stable and would hold up. Knowing what to sacrifice is part of knowing when something is done.

I've never shipped work I thought was structurally unstable. I've shipped work that wasn't as pretty as I'd have liked. There's a clear distinction between those two things and I hold the line on the structural one.
