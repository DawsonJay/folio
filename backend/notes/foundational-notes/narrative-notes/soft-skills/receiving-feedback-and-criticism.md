# Receiving Feedback and Criticism

I value feedback as essential to creating valuable work and improving as a developer.

## Feedback Philosophy

My approach to feedback comes from two sources: teaching kayaking and building the Integrations Dashboard at Nurtur.

**Feedback is about the work, not about me**: I focus on the outcome, not defending my approach. What matters is creating something that solves problems and provides value, not being right about how I initially approached it.

**Real feedback makes real value**: When building the Integrations Dashboard, I talked at length with both the sales team and backend team to find out what they needed and what they found most valuable as I worked. This real feedback and collaboration was vital to making something that provided real value. The result has been in production for 3+ years with zero maintenance, proving that feedback-driven development creates lasting solutions.

## How I Seek Feedback

**Early and often**: I don't wait until something is "done" to ask for feedback. I check in with stakeholders throughout development to make sure I'm on the right track.

**From multiple perspectives**: On the dashboard project, I sought feedback from both technical (backend team) and non-technical (sales team) stakeholders. Different perspectives reveal different problems and opportunities.

**Specific questions**: Instead of "what do you think?", I ask targeted questions like "does this workflow match how you actually use the system?" or "is this the information you need to make decisions?"

## Acting on Feedback

**Listen first**: As a kayaking instructor and when teaching CSS to backend developers, I learned to listen to what people actually need before explaining my perspective. This skill transfers to receiving technical feedback.

**Evaluate thoughtfully**: Not all feedback is equal. I consider the source's expertise, the problem they're trying to solve, and whether their suggestion aligns with the project's goals.

**Communicate changes**: When I act on feedback, I explain what I changed and why. When I don't, I explain my reasoning respectfully.

## Receiving Criticism

Criticism is harder than general feedback, but it's often more valuable. When someone points out a problem with my work:

**Assume good intent**: Most criticism comes from someone trying to help the project succeed, not from personal attack. Approaching it that way makes it easier to hear and act on.

**Ask clarifying questions**: If criticism is vague ("this doesn't work well"), I ask specific questions to understand the real concern. Often the critic knows something is wrong but can't articulate exactly what.

**Thank people for the feedback**: Even when criticism stings, I thank people for taking the time to point out problems. That encourages a culture where people feel comfortable being honest.

**Separate myself from the code**: My code isn't my identity. If someone says my code has problems, they're helping me improve the work, not attacking me personally.

## The Integrations Dashboard Example

The zero-maintenance success of the Integrations Dashboard came directly from feedback-driven development. I didn't assume I knew what the sales team needed - I asked, listened, iterated, and kept checking back.

When backend developers gave feedback about API design or data structure, I listened even when it meant changing my approach. Their perspective caught problems I couldn't see from the frontend perspective.

When sales team members said "this doesn't match our workflow," I didn't defend the design - I understood their workflow better and adjusted.

That openness to feedback is why the system still works perfectly years later. Feedback revealed the real requirements, not my assumptions about requirements.

## The Feedback Loop

The feedback loop is how I learn and improve. My best work comes from collaboration, not isolation.

This doesn't mean I accept all feedback uncritically. But I approach feedback as valuable information to evaluate, not as personal attack to defend against.

Teaching kayaking taught me that people perform better when they feel safe to make mistakes and receive feedback. I try to create that environment in technical teams as well - where feedback flows freely because everyone knows it's about making the work better, not about proving who's right.
