# Being the Only Expert in the Room: Sole Frontend Developer Experience

## The Clearest Examples

The clearest experiences of being the only domain expert were Integrations and Nexus at Nurtur. On each project I was the only frontend developer for the entire duration. On Nexus it was me and one backend developer (Craig); even the senior developer overseeing the project from a distance was backend-only. I was responsible for everything on the frontend: React, Material UI, CSS, architectural decisions, and design.

On Integrations I was working alongside an entire team of experienced backend developers in systems they'd known for years. The frontend perspective was exclusively mine. They were highly competent in their domain; I was the only person thinking about frontend behaviour, interface design, and how the sales team would actually experience the product.

## The Practical Reality

There were people I could escalate to when something was seriously wrong, but they weren't in the codebase day-to-day. Getting a second opinion meant someone putting in real effort to understand code they didn't know — it wasn't a casual lean-over-and-ask the way it is with a frontend colleague on the same project. For most decisions, I was working it out alone.

Over time this gave me a reputation as highly mobile. Frontend tasks that didn't justify a dedicated team tended to come to me. Backend developers would reach out informally when they had frontend questions. I was regularly moved between teams when a project needed frontend work done, which accumulated a wide range of context about how different parts of the company worked.

## What Was Genuinely Good

The freedom was real. I worked the way I thought best. Nobody was there to override architectural decisions I'd thought through carefully, and nobody introduced inconsistencies that I'd then have to reconcile. I had full ownership and the quality of the codebase reflected my choices directly.

Being valued for something no one else around me could do had a different quality than fitting into a larger team of peers. When colleagues came to me with frontend questions, or when I explained a design decision and it landed well, those felt like contributions that actually mattered rather than incremental additions to someone else's work.

## What Was Harder

Learning mostly from my own mistakes rather than a peer's eye on the same code. When a frontend colleague works in the same codebase, they naturally catch things — not just bugs, but approaches that could be simpler, patterns that don't fit the project's style, or solutions they've seen work well elsewhere. Without that, I grew from my own experience but missed the compounding effect of someone else's perspective.

The specific thing I missed most was being able to share a clever solution with someone who'd understand what I'd done. When I solved something in a way that felt elegant — a tricky CSS layout problem, a state management approach that kept the component tree clean, an API call pattern that handled race conditions gracefully — there was no one to share it with. The satisfaction was still there, but something about showing someone who'd appreciate the nuance was missing.

## What It Built

Working as the sole frontend developer multiple times built a level of self-reliance and breadth that's harder to develop in a fully-staffed team. I had to make architectural decisions that in larger teams would be made by a lead. I had to be confident about choices without external validation. I had to hold all the context about the frontend myself because there was no one else who held any of it. That forced a completeness of thinking that's genuinely useful.
