# What Drives Me as a Developer

## Core Motivation: Curiosity, Collaboration, and Craftsmanship

My fundamental values are curiosity (continuous learning and exploration), collaboration (building products that help teams and create value), and craftsmanship (taking pride in work and building things that last). These drive everything I do as a developer.

## Creating Things That Matter

The moment of stepping back and seeing something perfect: I love art and coding for the same reason - you spend hours in a creative frenzy working on a project, staying up until early morning trying to get one thing perfect. Then you step back and see this perfect thing that you made, something real and lasting. This is what I loved about art - the creativity, the hours spent in a creative frenzy on a project, staying up until the early hours of the morning trying to get this one thing perfect. I like being able to step back after all of that and see the perfect thing that you had made, something real and lasting.

But coding has an advantage over art: Success isn't determined by public vibes or subjective taste. It's determined by skill and the value the code produces. It's both creative and intellectual, satisfying multiple needs at once.

## Solving Problems Through Cleverness

"When resources are scarce, you have to turn to cleverness and creativity": I'm driven by the challenge of finding elegant solutions within constraints. The WhatNow project showed this - I had to solve the dataset problem through evolution from manual metadata to embeddings because I couldn't just collect millions of user interactions.

Innovation and practicality can be the same thing: The best solutions are both clever and simple. The Integrations Dashboard working for 3+ years without maintenance isn't because it's complicated - it's because it's well-thought-out and stable.

## Making Teams Better

Being valuable to my team: I'm not driven by solo glory. I want to be a driving force of creativity, passion, and solutions that makes the whole team stronger.

Sharing clever solutions: I learned from freelancing that I need teammates to share solutions with. I'm more motivated when I can show someone a clever approach and they understand why it's elegant.

Helping others discover their strength: The "team dad" leadership style isn't just natural - it's what motivates me. When I helped backend developers realize they could learn CSS, those moments of helping someone realize they're more capable than they thought drive me as much as solving technical problems.

## Learning and Growth

I'm always pursuing goals: Learning happens naturally when working toward meaningful objectives. I don't think you ever really master a skill - you just learn enough to reach your current goals. People who stop learning are the ones who stop making bigger, more exciting goals.

Self-directed learning: I taught myself Python to work with Arduinos, C# to make Unity games, Java before university. Each skill emerged from a goal that inspired me. This drive to teach myself whatever I need to achieve ambitious goals is fundamental to who I am.

Pushing to new heights: My career progression from web development → AI/ML integration is deliberate. I'm building toward work that aligns with my values while continuously expanding what I'm capable of.

## Values Alignment

I don't want to work for money alone: When I was offered an interview for freemium games, I ended it because I disagreed with the ethics. No matter how much I needed a job, I couldn't support something I didn't believe in.

The freemium games story is particularly meaningful to me. I was living with my parents, had no money, and was absolutely terrified of having to go back to my retail job and never getting back out. Months had passed like this while I sent my thin resume to any game dev company who'd take it. I wasn't a very good developer at this point, and I was getting more and more panicked. Eventually I got a response and an interview. The interview went well - I would be part of a team of game developers making a mobile game. They didn't seem to care about my lack of experience, just that I was breathing and could code. It was finally a chance to get some experience in game development. It was finally worth holding out for so long, for not giving up on my dream. I was being given a chance.

But then the conversation moved on to the kind of game and my stomach dropped. It was a freemium game. I know that doesn't sound like a big thing, like maybe I was disappointed by the lack of glamour, but that wasn't it. Freemium games are toxic - they use slot machine tactics to prey on the tiny percentage of players with that kind of addiction, and they take almost all of their profits from those few. They're predatory.

Games were sacred to me. They were a huge part of my childhood - glimpses into different lives, different worlds. Getting to pretend to be a hero, to go on adventures. They taught values and grit and imagination. Pokemon and Legend of Zelda had as much of an impact on me as any person did. I remember loving the idea of being in my treehouse base in Pokemon Sapphire, camping out with my team of Pokemon, planning out our next raid on Team Aqua. Of exploring the world, diving to the bottom of the ocean, fighting to the top of a volcano, utterly unstoppable. They were why I wanted to work on games in the first place - the chance to inspire someone else like that, to be a bright light in someone else's childhood. And like all stories, games help shape the culture. They're a very real force in making people want to be better. Freemium games went against all of that, preying on the people they were meant to inspire.

But this felt like my only chance. The one opportunity I might get to be part of the industry. It had already been months, and how much damage could one game do really? I declined. Even if it meant I never had another chance, I couldn't do it. I wanted to make games because they mattered to me, and this would go against all of that. It felt like giving up an entire future, but I ended the interview early and walked away. My stomach felt like it was filled with lead and acid. I couldn't do anything else that day, sure that I'd just destroyed my own future.

It's hard in the moment to know if you made the right call. My philosophy has always been that you can't choose your future, but you can choose who you are walking into it. I knew that accepting the job would have meant being someone I didn't like or respect anymore. It's the same choice I've made multiple times in my life - that quiet afterwards where the fear of not knowing what came next mixed with the pride of staying true to myself. It wasn't the end of that career. A few weeks later, I stumbled onto another job acting as a volunteer for a small games company, and this one I accepted.

Purpose-driven work: I want to build things that matter and have real impact. Technologies that solve real problems and create genuine value for users - those are what I want to build.

My career evolution should reflect my work coming more in line with my values: Each project should move me toward work that matters deeply to me. The Integrations Dashboard helped a sales team work better - collaboration and craftsmanship value. WhatNow helped people discover new experiences - curiosity and collaboration value. Building AI-powered applications that enhance user experiences.

## The Craft Itself

Intuition and elegance: My artistic background gave me intuition about when code structure feels right, when something is "done," when I'm on the wrong path. I love that feeling of the code clicking into place, of finding the elegant solution.

Results over process: I care about creating something excellent, not about being seen to work in a particular way. If I need to hike for 2 days with a notebook to think through a problem, that's valid because what matters is the work.

Building things that last: The Integrations Dashboard still providing value years later, with stable architecture that doesn't need constant maintenance - that's satisfying in a way that quick hacks never are.

## Long-term Drive

I want to work on projects that create genuine impact: Building AI-powered web applications that solve meaningful problems and enhance people's lives in tangible ways. Creating systems that provide real value and make a difference.

Making a difference: "I want people to know my name because we made something extraordinary and helpful and good that's making a difference." Not famous for being famous, but known for contributing to work that matters.

What drives me is the combination: creating elegant solutions to hard problems, helping my team be their best, continuously learning and growing, and building things that matter and last. When all of those align, I bring creativity, passion, and solutions that last.
