# My Journey from Art and Teaching to Technology

My journey into development started with art. I loved making things and creating, but I realized fine art was too dependent on my mood and public opinion to be a sustainable career. Coding gave me that same creative satisfaction - the joy of bringing ideas into reality - but with clear, objective parameters for success. Code either works or it doesn't. There's feedback, iteration, and craftsmanship, but the path to "good enough" is much clearer than it is in fine art. That clarity is what sparked my interest in tech.

I completed a Foundation Year in Illustration at the University of Derby, studying visual approaches, creative skills, and artistic techniques. This might seem like an unusual background for a software developer, but I've found that art and technology share more than people usually recognize. Both art and tech are fundamentally about taking an idea and making it real. In art, you have a vision and you figure out how to bring it into existence through drawing, painting, sculpture, or digital media. In technology, you have a concept and you figure out how to build it through code, architecture, and systems design. The creative process is remarkably similar - starting with something abstract, working through constraints and technical challenges, iterating based on what works, and ultimately producing something that exists in the world.

The transition from art to technology was driven by a deep realization about what I needed. I loved art - the creativity, the hours spent in a creative frenzy working on a project, staying up until early morning trying to get one thing perfect. I loved being able to step back after all of that and see this perfect thing that I had made, something real and lasting. But I didn't like how subjective art was, especially its value. I didn't like how dependent it was on the opinions of the general public whether I would be successful or not. I found a lot of the people were too wrapped up in their own egos and pretension. I also struggled with how little it exercised my intellectual side. It was very creative, but as someone who is both creative and logical, I needed more.

Leaving fine art was incredibly difficult. I know society would see it as a failure or laziness. My family wouldn't understand. It was a lot of uncertainty not knowing what I would do next. I was terrified by the idea of having to work in a co-op supermarket forever, and it was difficult having to move back in with my parents. But I knew fine art was wrong as a career for me. In the time after, I got interested in coding and realized that it fulfilled a lot of what I loved about art as well as fulfilling other needs such as my need for more intellectual challenges. I loved that as a more practical pursuit, coding success wasn't determined by public vibes, but by skill and the value a piece of code produced.

The transition happened through education, though not in the traditional way. I tried studying AI at Heriot Watt University but found it too slow and disconnected from reality. I had been coding for a few years by that point, and I had spent a year redoing my A-levels to get the qualifications I needed to apply for Heriot Watt. I knew that coding was the correct career path for me, but I realized that the university course wasn't helping me with that as a career.

I saw university as having three main goals: to get a qualification that would get me a good job, to teach me more about coding in a real-world way, and to let me connect with people my own age and try out different experiences and learn how to be an adult. University failed me on two of those. I researched the job market and found that while a lot of places liked you to have a qualification, what they really required was a portfolio of projects proving that you could do the work. I knew that I could create my own portfolio far quicker than the four-year course would, and I could save a lot of money on a degree that wasn't even needed.

I learned almost nothing. I knew Java before we started and learned very little else about it. We had an interesting course on boolean algebra that was fascinating, but just theoretical. I drew the line at the end of exams when I was given a paper on Python where we had to write code for a program on paper with no reference material or computer or internet access at all. This was so appallingly disconnected from the reality of coding that it broke my faith in the university being able to prepare me for a coding career.

On the third goal about connecting with people and becoming an adult, I did very well. I was popular, I joined lots of clubs, learned to manage my own finances and manage my own house, expanded my skills in cooking from just baking to being able to feed myself and stay healthy, learned to manage my time. But none of that was because of the university - I achieved success in that goal because I actively went out and did it. The university just provided a venue.

So with all of that in mind, I decided to leave the course again, which again was hard. I had to leave my friends and face disappointment from my family again. But now I had a plan and a career path that I was determined to follow. I did a bootcamp that promised portfolio-building and job readiness, and I got my first web development job at BriefYourMarket shortly after. That practical, hands-on approach has defined my career path ever since.

What my artistic background gave me is a different way of thinking about technical problems. I approach UI design with an understanding of visual balance, color theory, and how people interact with visual information. When I built the theatrical diorama system for my portfolio website - with its layered SVG animations and color gradients from dark tech surfaces to warm paper lantern tones - that came from my artistic foundation. I think about how systems look and feel to users, not just whether they function correctly.

My artistic background helps with intuition and a sense of when things are "done." As a coder, you get taught to rely purely on logic - it's seen as a very logical intellectual pursuit and in some ways it is, but it's also a creative endeavor. Being able to rely on accurate feelings means I can be sensitive to the underlying structure of the code, to be able to intuit when I'm on the wrong path and I need to fix the current problem in a new way. It means that I can follow an instinct on what might be causing a bug and usually be right. For investigations and awareness of the code on a more abstract, structural level, intuition is one of my most valuable skills. Obviously I follow up an intuition with a rigorous logical investigation - the feeling acts as a compass, not as proof.

It helps with knowing when something is done because when working on a painting, or throwing a pot, or taking a photo, there's a point, a sudden gut feeling when you know things are exactly right, that anything else you do will make things worse or be wasted effort. That's a skill it takes years to develop, and my artistic background did that for me. It's very useful to know when a piece of code is done, where it's stable and functional and well written, and adding any more polish is perfectionism as opposed to being practical. Finally, my art background helps with UX because UX is fundamentally the same as art - it's communicating a concept to a viewer through images and experience.

The creative foundation also influences how I think about component architecture and system design. There's an aesthetic to good code - clean composition, elegant solutions, systems that feel naturally structured rather than forced. When I describe my architecture philosophy as "crystal formation" - simple components combining into sophisticated structure - that's a visual metaphor that comes from my art background.

Kayaking was separate from my career goals. It's a sport I've done since childhood, and I started teaching it as a volunteer during my art and higher education courses as a way to give back to that community. I gave it up when I moved to Edinburgh, but the teaching experience profoundly influenced how I approach mentoring and leadership in tech. Teaching kayaking taught me how to support people's growth, how to create safe spaces for learning, and how to help people discover their own capabilities rather than just instructing them. Those lessons carry directly into how I work with junior developers and how I approach team leadership.

The narrative that connects all my experiences is about creation, problem-solving, and helping others. Art taught me creativity, intuition, and how to work toward a vision through iteration and experimentation. Teaching kayaking taught me how to support growth, communicate clearly about complex skills, and create environments where people feel safe to try and fail. Both experiences inform how I work as a developer - I bring creative problem-solving to technical challenges, I have intuitive understanding of code structure and project health, and my leadership style is focused on supporting and elevating teammates rather than controlling or directing them.

All these experiences shaped me into someone who builds things that last, who communicates effectively across different teams and contexts, and who helps others grow. Whether I'm writing code, designing architecture, or mentoring a colleague, the thread that runs through everything is the same: making things, solving problems, and lifting people up. The path from art and teaching to technology might look unusual, but to me it feels like a natural evolution of the same core drives - just expressed through different mediums and applied to different challenges.

I don't think of myself as an artist who learned programming or a programmer with an art background. I'm someone who found that art and technology are different expressions of the same underlying drive - taking ideas and making them real in the world. The creative problem-solving, visual thinking, and appreciation for elegance I developed through art inform everything I build in technology.

When employers see my background, some might worry about whether someone who started in art can handle rigorous technical work. My portfolio of production systems, complex algorithms, and deployed applications demonstrates that the transition is complete. But I haven't left the art behind - I've integrated it into how I approach technology, and I think that makes me a better developer because I think about both how systems work and how they feel to use.
