# Learning to Code: Self-Taught Journey

My journey into coding started long before I entered professional development. I began teaching myself programming around 2014, well before I considered it as a career path. This self-taught foundation shaped how I approach learning and problem-solving in ways that continue to influence my work today.

I started with Java because I wanted to understand AI concepts. At the time, I was interested in artificial intelligence and machine learning, and Java seemed like a good entry point. I didn't take any courses or follow structured tutorials - I just started reading books, trying things out, and building small projects. This exploratory approach taught me to learn by doing rather than by studying, which has become my preferred learning style.

Python came next when I wanted to work with Arduinos and embedded systems. I was fascinated by the idea of programming physical devices, making sensors respond to the environment, and building things that interacted with the real world. Python's simplicity made it accessible for rapid experimentation. I learned by building projects that required specific capabilities - when I needed to read sensor data, I learned how to do that. When I needed to control motors, I learned that. The goal-driven approach meant I learned exactly what I needed, when I needed it.

C# entered my toolkit when I wanted to develop games with Unity. Game development appealed to me because it combined creative expression with technical problem-solving. I taught myself C# specifically to work with Unity, learning the language through building actual game prototypes rather than following tutorials. This pattern of learning through building meaningful projects has defined my entire approach to skill development.

The self-taught period wasn't structured or formal. I read books, did courses on Codecademy, followed along with Udemy courses, and built half-formed games and projects. I didn't have a clear curriculum or learning path - I just followed my curiosity and built things that interested me. This unstructured approach had both advantages and disadvantages. On one hand, I learned to be resourceful and figure things out independently. On the other hand, I missed some foundational concepts that would have been covered in structured education.

What I learned during this period wasn't just programming languages - it was how to learn. I developed the ability to read documentation, understand error messages, debug problems systematically, and persist through frustration. These meta-skills are arguably more valuable than any specific language knowledge because they transfer to every new technology I encounter.

The self-taught approach also taught me to value practical results over theoretical understanding. I cared less about understanding every detail of how something worked internally and more about whether I could make it do what I needed. This pragmatic focus has served me well in professional development where shipping working code matters more than perfect theoretical knowledge.

When I eventually entered formal education (Heriot-Watt University's AI program), I found the academic pace frustratingly slow compared to my self-taught learning speed. I had already learned Java before university, and the coursework felt like it was holding me back rather than accelerating my growth. However, the university experience wasn't wasted - I completed 2 years of coursework, earned a Certificate of Merit for my first year performance, and gained foundational knowledge in machine learning, neural networks, algorithms, and databases that would later inform my work on AI/ML projects like WhatNow, moh-ami, and Folio.

The contrast between my self-directed learning pace and the structured academic pace was a key factor in my decision to leave university and pursue a more practical path. I realized that traditional university structure wasn't the right learning environment for my goal-driven approach. I needed project-based learning where I could apply concepts immediately rather than studying theory in isolation. This realization led me to the bootcamp path, which provided the practical, hands-on learning that aligned with how I learn best.

The bootcamp experience validated my self-taught foundation. While other students were learning programming concepts for the first time, I was building on years of prior experience. This allowed me to focus on professional practices - working in teams, code reviews, deployment, real project constraints - rather than basic syntax. The self-taught period gave me a head start that made bootcamp more valuable.

Even after entering professional development, I've continued the self-taught approach for new technologies. TypeScript, Redux Toolkit, machine learning concepts, LLM integration, embedded systems, RAG systems - all learned through building projects that required them. I don't wait for training or permission to learn something new. If a project needs a technology I don't know, I learn it. This adaptability is one of my strongest professional assets.

The self-taught learning style has shaped my entire career trajectory. I'm goal-driven rather than curriculum-driven. I learn best through meaningful projects, not abstract study. I value practical results and working systems over theoretical perfection. These characteristics make me well-suited for roles where I need to learn quickly and adapt to new requirements.

What I've learned from the self-taught journey is that learning is a skill itself. The ability to teach yourself new technologies quickly is more valuable than knowing any specific technology. In a field that changes rapidly, the developers who thrive are those who can learn continuously and independently. My self-taught foundation gave me that capability, and it's something I continue to develop with every new project.

The self-taught period also taught me to trust my ability to figure things out. When I encounter a problem I don't know how to solve, I don't panic or assume I can't do it. I know from experience that I can learn what I need to solve the problem. This confidence enables me to take on challenges that might intimidate others.

Looking back, the self-taught journey from 2014 to 2020 was crucial preparation for professional development. It gave me technical skills, learning skills, problem-solving skills, and confidence. But more importantly, it established a pattern of continuous learning and self-directed growth that continues to drive my career development today.
