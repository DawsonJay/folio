# Ethics and Red Lines: Where I Stand and Why

## The Baseline Position

My position on working on products with ethically complicated applications is case-dependent with clear, non-negotiable red lines. I don't have a blanket refusal for defence or dual-use work, and I don't have a blanket acceptance either. What it actually is depends on what that particular product is doing and who it's doing it for.

For a military not committing atrocities — even one involved in a conflict I'd consider just or defensive — I'm comfortable with that. I don't see it as categorically different from paying taxes that fund a military. The world is complicated enough that treating all militaries and all defence work as equivalent moral territory is a simplification I don't think holds up.

## The Red Lines

The red lines are about specific conduct: genocide, torture, mass civilian casualties. If a company's product was materially supporting a government committing those kinds of crimes, I would refuse the work — not just flag it internally and carry on, but refuse it. These lines are rare to approach in normal software work, which is why I can draw them precisely: they're extreme situations, not everyday political disagreements.

When I object to a regime it's about conduct and material complicity, not the ethnicity or background of anyone I work with. Proportionality matters enormously: there's a massive gap between a colleague's cultural identity and materially enabling serious wrongdoing. I'm only talking about the latter. I won't campaign in the office or have loud political discussions, but I wouldn't stay at a company whose actions crossed those kinds of rare, material lines.

## When Uncomfortable Use Cases Arise

My approach when I discover a feature I'm building has a use case I'm uncomfortable with is a clear escalation path, not unilateral action.

First, I check with a trusted colleague — sometimes you're seeing something that isn't there, and a sanity check matters. If the concern has substance, I raise it with my manager: here's the issue, here's a possible way to address it. I propose solutions, not just problems. If that's refused and the concern is serious, I go higher. If that also fails, I resign.

What I won't do is compromise on the feature or sabotage it. I have a contract with my employer and violating it would go against my own ethics as strongly as the original concern. If I'm responsible for shipping, I ship — even while escalating. Removing myself at the critical moment when leaving would compromise the delivery is, to me, the same as sabotage. I raise the issue throughout, ship what I'm accountable for, leave immediately after if that's where things stand, and continue to advocate for changing the product after leaving.

## A Concrete Earlier Example

Before I had professional experience I got a promising interview at a game studio. It went well and he seemed to like me. Then he revealed it was for a freemium game. I'm against freemium games: they use slot machine mechanics that target addiction, and like slot machines they make almost all their profits from a small number of people who can't afford it. I politely ended the interview. That was an early version of the same pattern: clear position, proportionate response, no drama.

## What This Isn't

This isn't political campaigning or moralising at colleagues. These positions are reserved for genuinely serious situations — not everyday business decisions I happen to disagree with, not work I find boring or that doesn't align with my interests. I'm talking about a narrow category of extreme cases that most software work never comes close to.
