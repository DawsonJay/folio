# Have you mentored other developers?

Yes, I've successfully mentored 3 backend developers at Nurtur who wanted to expand their skills into frontend development. One of them had never worked with CSS before and was intimidated by it. We did pair programming sessions where I'd explain CSS concepts by relating them to backend patterns they already understood - "It's like selecting data from a database, but you're selecting elements from the DOM." That connection helped it click for them. Now all three of them work confidently across the full stack, which has made our team more flexible and effective.

What I've learned about teaching is that it's not about dumping information on someone. It's about understanding where they're starting from, what they're trying to achieve, and building a bridge between those two points. I try to make people feel rock-solid confidence in their ability to learn something, even if it seems difficult at first. Fear and insecurity are the biggest barriers to learning, not capability.

My approach to mentoring is patient and encouraging. I'll work through fundamentals, build understanding step by step, and celebrate improvement rather than fixating on perfection. When someone asks a question, I try to answer in a way that doesn't just solve their immediate problem but helps them understand the underlying principles so they can solve similar problems themselves in the future.

Beyond formal mentoring, I naturally end up in a connecting role on teams. I bridge gaps between people - helping backend developers understand what frontend developers need, translating technical constraints into language non-technical stakeholders can understand, and generally making sure people are communicating effectively. I've found that most conflicts come from misunderstanding rather than genuine disagreement, and often just facilitating clear communication resolves things.

My communication style focuses on making complex concepts accessible. I relate technical concepts to familiar experiences - explaining CSS selectors like database queries, describing state management like organizing a bookshelf, comparing API calls to sending letters. These analogies help people understand abstract concepts by connecting them to things they already know.

I also focus on active listening and understanding others' perspectives before presenting my own. When someone is struggling with a problem, I first try to understand their mental model and where they're getting stuck. Then I can provide guidance that builds on their existing understanding rather than replacing it with my approach.

What excites me about leadership is seeing people grow. Watching those three backend developers gain confidence in frontend development and seeing them build features they couldn't have tackled before - that's incredibly satisfying. I'd rather help create ten bright sparks of confidence and capability in others than shine brightly alone.

The teaching experience from kayaking profoundly influenced how I approach mentoring and leadership in tech. Teaching kayaking taught me how to support people's growth, how to create safe spaces for learning, and how to help people discover their own capabilities rather than just instructing them. Those lessons carry directly into how I work with junior developers and how I approach team leadership.
