# How do you handle disagreements with teammates?

I've never had a major disagreement because I'm very good at managing them before they blow up into anything bigger. My philosophy is that we all want the same thing in a team - to make a project that works well and gives value. Any disagreement after that is either a lack of understanding or communication.

This isn't about avoiding conflict. It's about recognizing that most disagreements come from miscommunication or different perspectives on the same goal, not from fundamental conflicts. When everyone wants the project to succeed, disagreements are usually solvable through better understanding.

I always listen to the other person's view and experience, and then give my own, and that's always cleared things up. Most of the time, when people understand each other's perspectives and reasoning, the disagreement resolves itself. What seemed like a conflict was actually just different information or different experiences leading to different conclusions.

When I listen first, I'm not just waiting for my turn to talk. I'm trying to understand: What problem are they trying to solve? What information do they have that I don't? What experiences led them to their conclusion? What are they actually concerned about?

Then when I explain my perspective, I'm connecting it to their concerns. I'm showing how my approach addresses what they care about, or explaining why I think a different approach is better given the constraints we both understand.

Sometimes there's still a disagreement after we've both listened and explained. Then it comes down to authority. If I think something should be done a certain way and the team lead disagrees, as long as I've given them all the relevant information, the decision is theirs and I'll accept it because they own the project and the responsibility for it.

This is about respect for ownership and responsibility. If someone owns the project, they own the decisions. I can provide information, perspective, and reasoning, but if they make a different decision after hearing everything, I'll execute their decision professionally. They're the ones who will bear the consequences, so they get to make the call.

When we both have the same authority, I'll still listen, but if it's an area where I have more experience, I'll go forward with my own approach and they'll accept it. It's about recognizing expertise. If I've built similar systems before and understand the tradeoffs, and someone else hasn't, it makes sense for me to lead in that area.

I address disagreements early, when they're small and solvable. By listening first, I often discover that what seemed like a disagreement was actually just missing information. By explaining my reasoning, I help others understand my perspective. By respecting authority and expertise, I avoid power struggles that don't serve the project.

The result is that disagreements become problem-solving conversations rather than conflicts. We're working together to find the best solution, not fighting to win an argument. That's the kind of team environment where good work happens.
