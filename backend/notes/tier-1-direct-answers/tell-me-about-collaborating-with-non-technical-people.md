# Tell me about collaborating with non-technical people

I'm good at explaining complex technical concepts by relating them to familiar experiences. My background in art, teaching kayaking, and bridging teams at Nurtur has developed this skill.

The Integrations Dashboard required collaborating with the sales team who weren't technical. My strategy: Focus on what they need to accomplish, not how the code works. Show them the interface and workflow, get feedback on usability. Explain technical constraints in terms of business impact. Ask questions about their process before suggesting technical solutions.

The backend developers and sales team had completely different perspectives and communication styles. The sales team spent all day talking to customers and working with spreadsheets. The backend team worked with Azure functions and C# code. They thought in different terms even about the same subject, which created communication challenges.

I could talk to both teams because of my coding background combined with more social pursuits like art, kayaking, and teaching. I explained backend constraints to the sales team in terms of their workflow and customer needs. I translated sales requests to the backend team in terms of data structures and API requirements.

I use metaphors and analogies. I relate technical concepts to familiar things: comparing code organization to physical objects people understand, using visual metaphors from art to explain abstract technical patterns, drawing parallels between technical systems and natural systems.

I approach communication by listening first. In teaching, pair programming, or cross-team collaboration, I make sure I understand someone's actual problem before offering solutions. Often what people think they need isn't what they actually need, and listening reveals the real issue.

I make people feel it's okay to not know. The biggest barrier to learning is feeling like you'll never understand it. I let people know we all felt like them at the start. This psychological safety allows actual learning to happen.

I teach basic principles and work up. Rather than overwhelming with everything at once, I show fundamentals and how to frame problems. Once someone understands the foundation, they can build their own knowledge.

The ability to communicate across different contexts - technical to non-technical, backend to frontend, developer to user - is one of my strongest skills. It comes from understanding that good communication isn't about showing how much you know, it's about helping others understand what they need to know.
